🌊 National Natural Monument No. 437: The Jeongdongjin Coastal Terrace of Gangneung
🧭 2.86 km of sheer oceanic majesty linking Jeongdongjin Sun Cruise to the serene fishing harbor of Simgok

🌊 Primordial Edge of the Sea: The breathtaking wooden boardwalk clinging to vertical ocean cliffs along Gangneung's Badabuchaegil / Photo by Paeton

For over six decades following the Korean War, the dramatic coastal cliffs stretching between Jeongdongjin and Simgok Port in Gangneung remained shrouded behind razor wire and military barricades. Accessible only to armed patrol guards protecting the maritime borders of the Republic of Korea, this pristine marine sanctuary was strictly concealed from civilian footsteps. In October 2017, after meticulous ecological planning, this legendary barrier was finally opened to the public under the evocative name: Jeongdong-Simgok Badabuchaegil (정동·심곡 바다부채길).

The name itself paints a profound geographical portrait: seen from the rolling swells of the open sea looking back toward the land, the topography unfurls like a grand traditional Korean folding fan (Buchae, 부채) welcoming the maritime horizon. Spanning 2.86 kilometers of uninterrupted steel decks and wooden catwalks suspended directly above roaring sapphire waters, this spectacular marine trail allows travelers to commune directly with Natural Monument No. 437—Korea's sole and most expansive coastal terrace formed 23 million years ago.

💎 The Eternal Symphony of Surge: Crystal azure swells of the East Sea crashing against wave-cut rock terraces / Photo by Paeton

1. A Geological Masterpiece: 23 Million Years of Crustal Awakening

Unlike ordinary beach promenades or harbor piers, walking Badabuchaegil is an immersive journey through geological deep time. Approximately 23 million years ago, during the mid-Tertiary Period of the Cenozoic Era, cataclysmic tectonic activity caused the Korean Peninsula’s eastern crust to violently tilt and uplift while the sea bed subsided, ultimately carving the magnificent East Sea basin.

As ancient wave-cut platforms were repeatedly pushed high above sea level and sculpted by millennial wind and sea foam, stepped staircases of flat rock—known geologically as coastal terraces (해안단구, 海岸段丘)—were forged. Walking along the suspended deck, visitors can gaze straight down into subterranean tide pools where layers of prehistoric sedimentary rock lie exposed, perfectly preserved as if the ancient seabed were breathing right beneath one's boots.

📜 Written in Stone: Multi-tiered wave-cut terraces recording millions of years of tectonic upheaval and oceanic tides / Photo by Paeton

2. Sculpted Legends: Buchaebawi and the Mythic Guardian Pillars

As travelers proceed along the undulating cliffline, the path unfolds a gallery of monumental natural rock sculptures. Foremost among them is Buchaebawi (Fan Rock, 부채바위), a majestic outcrop that resembles a ceremonial fan unfurling its pleated ribs against the breaking surf.

Local lore dating back to the Joseon Dynasty whispers of an elderly villager from Simgok who had a vivid dream: a goddess with two mysterious paintings appeared to him, instructing him to enshrine them on the rocky cliffs. When he sailed his wooden boat out to the rocks, he miraculously discovered sacred portraits carved on stone. From that day forth, the villagers held annual sea rituals (Seonghwangje) at Buchaebawi, praying for abundant catches and the safe return of deep-sea fishermen. Today, standing upon the observation platform atop Buchaebawi, one feels enveloped in this timeless spiritual communion with the sea.

🪭 The Sacred Fan Rock (Buchaebawi): Rising like an immortal fan from the sea, where ancient fishermen prayed for calm waters / Photo by Paeton

3. Tugu Rock and the Sensory Thrill of Open-Air Catwalks

Further along the route rises Tugubawi (Helmet Rock, 투구바위), an imposing stone monolith shaped unmistakably like the heavy iron helmet and resolute facial profile of an ancient warrior standing vigil over the coast. According to Gangneung folklore, General Gang Gam-chan (강감찬 장군) of the Goryeo Dynasty once subdued mischievous goblins and mountain spirits right at this promontory, commanding them to protect the maritime approaches of the eastern realm forever.

What elevates Badabuchaegil beyond conventional coastal treks is the visceral sensation of the engineering itself. Portions of the trail feature open-grate steel decks where the frothing surf can be seen swirling violently beneath your feet, while sea breezes carry the refreshing, mineral-rich fragrance of the pine forests that blanket the slopes above.

🧭 Essential Visitor Guide: Jeongdong-Simgok Badabuchaegil

  • Trail Span: 2.86 km one-way (approx. 70 minutes at a leisurely photography pace)
  • Trailhead Locations:
    • Jeongdongjin Gate: Near Sun Cruise Resort, Jeongdongjin-ri, Gangdong-myeon, Gangneung-si
    • Simgok Gate: Simgok Harbor, 114-3 Simgok-ri, Gangdong-myeon, Gangneung-si
  • Terrain: Well-maintained timber and steel grating boardwalks, gentle coastal staircases (moderate fitness recommended)
  • Operating Hours: Summer (April–October) 09:00 – 17:30 (Last entry 16:30) / Winter (November–March) 09:00 – 16:30 (Last entry 15:30)
  • Admission: Adults 3,000 KRW / Youths 2,000 KRW / Children 1,500 KRW
  • Return Transit: Local circular shuttle buses and taxis connect Jeongdongjin Gate and Simgok Harbor frequently, allowing a hassle-free one-way hike without backtracking.
  • Weather Notice: For visitor safety, the trail closes automatically during severe ocean gale warnings, typhoons, or heavy storm surges. Check local municipal advisories before embarking.

As the trail gently winds into the quaint harbor of Simgok—a sheltered coastal nook nestled deeply between coastal ridges—one feels a rare sense of renewal. To step along Badabuchaegil is to walk where wind, sea, and tectonic rock have held their unbroken dialogue for over twenty million years. It stands proudly as one of the crowning achievements of Gangneung’s natural heritage and an indispensable destination for any world traveler seeking the authentic soul of Korea's Eastern Shore.
